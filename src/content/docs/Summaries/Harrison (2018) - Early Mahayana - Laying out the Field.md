## Abstract

Written as the revised opening address to the 2012 UKABS Cardiff symposium in honour of Sara Boin-Webb, Paul Harrison surveys the key debates and methodological challenges in the study of early Mahāyāna Buddhism. Moving past the outdated mid-twentieth-century stereotype of a "Theravāda–Mahāyāna split" driven by a lay revolt against monastic privilege, Harrison examines five major areas — the "forest hypothesis", the rhetorical role of lay figures, sūtra periodisation and intertextuality, *nikāya* affiliation, and archaeological/manuscript evidence — and argues that recent discoveries from Greater Gandhāra challenge the thesis that early Mahāyāna was merely a marginal phenomenon in India.

## 1. Beyond the "Theravāda–Mahāyāna Split"

* **Old consensus overturned**: Early twentieth-century scholars (e.g., Hirakawa, Lamotte, Conze, building on Burnouf and Rhys Davids) portrayed Mahāyāna as an institutionally separate, lay-led backlash against monastic self-absorption — a view still entrenched in popular and undergraduate thinking as a binary "Theravāda–Mahāyāna split".
* **Newer scholarly picture**: Since the 1960s–1970s (following Heinz Bechert and others), scholars have viewed early Mahāyāna as a pluralistic set of interrelated doctrinal ideas, ritual practices, and literary forms operating *across* all existing *nikāyas* (ordination lineages), primarily among renunciants pursuing demanding forms of self-engagement rather than a wholesale turn to lay devotion.

## 2. The "Forest Hypothesis" (`araṇyavāsa`)

* **Origin and limits**: Scholars such as Reginald Ray (1994), Jonathan Silk, Jan Nattier, Daniel Boucher, and Harrison himself explored the idea that early Mahāyāna arose among rigorous forest-dwelling ascetics (*araṇyavāsins*) practising the *dhūtaguṇas*. Harrison argues Ray went too far in treating forest ascetics as a separate "third force" outside the *vinaya*; rather, they were *bhikṣus* (and possibly *bhikṣuṇīs*) in good standing pursuing an austere lifestyle.
* **Moving closer to Drewes**: While noting David Drewes's critique of the forest hypothesis, Harrison concedes two points: (1) early Mahāyāna was not a single movement, so *araṇyavāsin* groups cannot be the whole story; and (2) the English translation "forest" misleads — *araṇya* in ancient India often meant a monastery slightly removed from towns (like the hilltop ruins of Takht-i-Bahi above the Peshawar plain), not an isolated hermit's hut. Similarly, "meditation" in Mahāyāna sūtras often refers to a form of textual practice.

## 3. The Role of the Laity

* Even after rejecting the old "lay revolt" theory, scholars still face a hermeneutical puzzle: why do prominent householders (*gṛhin* / *gṛhapati*) such as Ugra, Bhadrapāla, and Vimalakīrti figure so prominently in early Mahāyāna sūtras?
* Rather than reflecting social reality on the ground, these figures served diverse rhetorical agendas — whether as a monastic polemical tool (Vimalakīrti), a way to co-opt lay partners into textual practices (Ugra), or a strategy of *captatio benevolentiae* aimed at securing lay patronage (Bhadrapāla).

## 4. Sūtra Periodisation and Intertextuality

* **Beyond Chinese translation dates**: Scholarship has moved past relying disproportionately on the *Saddharmapuṇḍarīka* (*Lotus Sūtra*, which Harrison considers unlikely to be an early Mahāyāna sūtra) and must now overcome the "tyranny" of Chinese translation dates to build a systematic internal chronology of Mahāyāna sūtras and *śāstras*.
* **Case study — the *Sūtrasamuccaya***: Harrison shows that the *Sūtrasamuccaya* attributed to Nāgārjuna cites at least fourteen sūtras (including texts linked to the *Mahāsaṃnipāta* compendium) that only reached China in the fifth to tenth centuries and are never cited in Kumārajīva's early fifth-century encyclopaedic commentaries (*Da zhidu lun* and *Shizhu piposha lun*), making Nāgārjuna's authorship highly improbable.
* **Mapping intertextuality**: Mahāyāna sūtras exhibit high intertextuality (e.g., the Bajaur Mahāyāna Sūtra and *Vimalakīrtinirdeśa* presupposing the *Akṣobhyavyūha*; the *Mahāparinirvāṇa-mahāsūtra* drawing on the *Lokānuvartanā*). Mapping these cross-references alongside Chinese translation data is essential to reconstructing early Mahāyāna history.

## 5. *Nikāya* Affiliation and Mainstream Interaction

* **Cross-*nikāya* presence**: Early Mahāyāna was not an exclusive outgrowth of the Mahāsāṃghikas; it ran across *nikāya* boundaries from the beginning.
* **Simultaneous, mutual development**: Scholars must not assume that everything in the "Mainstream" (*Śrāvakayāna*) canons predates everything in Mahāyāna. Mainstream and Mahāyāna texts developed simultaneously and influenced one another — especially in Abhidharma — making the direction of influence in "proto-Mahāyāna" passages difficult to determine.

## 6. Material, Epigraphic, and Manuscript Evidence

* **Reassessing Schopen's marginality thesis**: Gregory Schopen's influential 1979 and 1987 inscription studies painted Mahāyāna as marginal in India until the fifth or sixth century. Harrison critiques Schopen's reading of *paramopāsaka* (which likely means "supreme lay follower", i.e. a ruler or dignitary, rather than a generic Mahāyāna layperson) and his claim that the 153 CE Govindnagar Amitābha pedestal's formula *sarvabuddhapūjāye* indicates non-Mahāyāna Sarvāstivādins (ignoring that Sarvāstivādins could also be Mahāyānists).
* **New epigraphic finds**: Discoveries from Greater Gandhāra and Central Asia undermine the marginality thesis, including the mid-third-century Endere inscription calling King Aṃgoka *mahāyānasaṃprasthita* ("set out in the Great Vehicle"), a fourth-century manuscript applying the same epithet to the second-century Kuṣāṇa king Huviṣka, and a 492/493 CE copper scroll mentioning Alchon Hun rulers and quoting both a Mahāyāna sūtra and Nāgārjuna's *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā*.
* **Gandhāran art ("the elephant in the room")**: Hundreds of Gandhāran bodhisattva statues and complex narrative steles (studied by Harrison and Christian Luczanits, building on the Muhammad Nari stele) attest to a widespread Mahāyāna presence in northwestern India.
* **Gāndhārī manuscripts (1st–3rd centuries CE)**: By 2018, nine Mahāyāna sūtras in Kharoṣṭhī/Gāndhārī had come to light — six known texts (*Prajñāpāramitā*, *Bodhisattvapiṭaka*, *Bhadrakalpika*, *Sarvapuṇyasamuccayasamādhi*, *\*Sucinti*, *Pratyutpanna*) and three previously unknown texts (including the extensive Bajaur Mahāyāna Sūtra). Their sophistication and the discovery of texts unknown in Chinese or Tibetan canons point to an "iceberg phenomenon": an enormous body of early Mahāyāna literature once existed in northwestern India.

## 7. Closing Remarks

Playing on the double meaning of "laying out the field" (both mapping a *kṣetravyūha* and preparing a body for burial), Harrison concludes that early Mahāyāna studies are far from dead. However, Mahāyāna has no *svabhāva* (unitary essence): it must be understood not as a sudden schism, but as a nexus of impulses within the broader matrix of Indian Buddhism and Indian culture, with continuities stretching both backward to early Buddhism and forward to Vajrayāna.
