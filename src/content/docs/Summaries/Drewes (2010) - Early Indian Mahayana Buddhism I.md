## Abstract

David Drewes surveys and evaluates major shifts in recent scholarship on early Indian Mahāyāna Buddhism. Long-standing twentieth-century theories — that Mahāyāna arose as a lay reaction against a "selfish" monastic `arhat` ideal, or as an offshoot of the Mahāsāṃghika monastic lineage — have been largely abandoned. In their place, scholars have proposed newer models linking early Mahāyāna to a "cult of the book", multiple sūtra-based cults, and forest-dwelling ascetics. Drewes assesses which recent conclusions are well founded and argues that several of the newer hypotheses are themselves problematic.

## 1. Collapse of Twentieth-Century Origin Theories

* **The lay-origin theory**: Rooted in nineteenth-century ideas (Vasil'ev 1857; Rhys Davids 1881) and synthesised without evidentiary support by Jean Przyluski (1926–1934), this theory claimed Mahāyāna began as a lay, altruistic reaction against monastic `arhat` ideals. It became dominant in the West via Étienne Lamotte and Edward Conze, while Akira Hirakawa developed a separate Japanese version locating Mahāyāna origins among lay groups around *stūpas*.
* **Paul Harrison's turning point (1987)**: Examining the earliest datable Mahāyāna texts — eleven second-century CE Chinese translations mostly attributed to Lokakṣema — Harrison showed that they place higher value on monasticism than lay life, do not seek a new sectarian identity, often accept `arhatship` as a valid goal, do not advocate worship of celestial bodhisattvas (such as Avalokiteśvara or Mañjuśrī), and are generally negative towards women's practice ("although both men and women can ride in the Great Vehicle, only men are allowed to drive it"). This effectively ended the lay-origin theory in the West, while scholars also critiqued Hirakawa and Lamotte for projecting modern Japanese lay Buddhism or European Protestant/secularist categories (*laïcité*) onto ancient India.
* **The Mahāsāṃghika-origin theory**: Popular since the late nineteenth century (Kern, Waddell, Rhys Davids), this theory collapsed as scholars recognised that Mahāyāna was not an institutionally separate sect splitting off from the *nikāyas*. Following Heinz Bechert, Paul Harrison, and Jonathan Silk (1994, 2002), the consensus is that Indian Mahāyāna monastics took ordination within existing *nikāyas* and often shared the same monasteries with non-Mahāyānists — a pan-Buddhist movement cutting across sectarian boundaries rather than a distinct order.

## 2. Established Recent Findings

Drewes identifies four major recent conclusions as likely to endure:

1. **End of the lay-origin theory** (Harrison 1987), along with the findings that early Mahāyāna sūtras do not advocate celestial bodhisattva worship, often approve of `arhatship`, and generally subordinate women.
2. **No separate Mahāyāna monastic order** (Bechert, Harrison, Silk): Mahāyānists were embedded within existing *nikāya* ordination lineages.
3. **Shared monasteries**: Chinese pilgrims and Indian texts alike show Mahāyānists and non-Mahāyānists living together in the same monasteries.
4. **Scarcity of early material evidence** (Schopen 1979, 1987): Apart from a single pedestal inscription of Amitābha dated to c. 153 CE (and two third-century Central Asian references), no clear Indian Mahāyāna epigraphy appears until the fourth or fifth century CE. As Schopen concluded, Mahāyāna entered the public domain "not with a bang, but a whimper", remaining a small minority movement without documented public support for centuries.

## 3. Critiques of Newer Hypotheses

Drewes argues that three prominent newer theories are not well supported by the evidence:

* **The "cult of the book" and written-tradition thesis**: Gregory Schopen (1975) argued from the phrase *sa pṛthivīpradeśaś caityabhūto bhavet* in the *Vajracchedikā* and other sūtras that early Mahāyāna rejected *stūpa* worship in favour of shrines enshrining manuscripts; Richard Gombrich (1988), K. R. Norman (1997), and David McMahan (2002) built on this to argue that writing enabled Mahāyāna's survival. Drewes counters that:
  * Contextually, *caityabhūta* is simply a simile ("like a shrine"), not a reference to physical book shrines, and the texts actually view *stūpa* worship positively.
  * No Mahāyāna manuscripts have been found in South Asian *stūpas* (the Gilgit manuscripts were found in the ruins of a house, not a *stūpa*), whereas many non-Mahāyāna texts have.
  * Early Mahāyāna sūtras emphasise oral/mnemic practices more frequently and with higher prestige than written copying, and Chinese translations show oral transmission.
  * Radiocarbon-dated non-Mahāyāna Gāndhārī manuscripts (2nd–1st century BCE) show writing was already used before Mahāyāna emerged; Indian traditions are better described as "literate orality" than strictly oral vs. written.
* **Multiple separate sūtra-cults**: Schopen (1975), Paul Williams (1989), and Jan Nattier (2003) proposed that early Mahāyāna consisted of many disconnected communities centred on individual sūtras. Drewes argues there is no evidence of distinct communities at all; doctrinal diversity across sūtras simply reflects different authors and a movement tolerant of theoretical innovation, as shown by early sūtra anthologies, *śāstras*, translators like Lokakṣema, and the *Aṣṭasāhasrikā Prajñāpāramitā* recommending other Mahāyāna sūtras.
* **The "forest hypothesis"**: Proposed by Harrison (1995), Reginald Ray (1994), and Nattier (2003), this model attributes early Mahāyāna to rigorous forest-dwelling ascetics (*araṇyavāsins*). Drewes contends that the sūtras themselves do not support it:
  * Only two of Lokakṣema's texts (*Pratyutpanna* and *Kāśyapaparivarta*) advocate forest dwelling; others ignore it, offer easy Pure Land practices (*Akṣobhyavyūha*, larger *Sukhāvatīvyūha*), or explicitly discourage it (the *Aṣṭasāhasrikā* calls forest dwelling a dangerous practice recommended by Māra).
  * Ray relies on only four translated texts, misreading the *Ratnaguṇasamcayagāthā*, which actually discourages forest dwelling.
  * Nattier's reliance on the *Ugraparipṛcchā* overlooks its likely later date and inconsistent stance on forest dwelling and lay superiority, as well as the twelve mostly easy methods for rebirth in Abhirati found in the earliest Chinese translation of the *Akṣobhyavyūha*.
