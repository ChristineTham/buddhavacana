## Abstract

Since the late twentieth century, an influential scholarly consensus — advanced by Gregory Schopen, Jan Nattier, Donald Lopez, and Jonathan Silk — has held that early Mahāyāna was not a single movement but a loose collection of independent, often competing communities or cults ("Mahāyānas" in the plural), each centred on an individual sūtra, which only later merged into a generalised "Mahāyāna". David Drewes challenges this paradigm, demonstrating that from its earliest surviving texts, Mahāyāna sūtras routinely endorsed *other* Mahāyāna sūtras as a shared corpus and expressed a unified millenarian self-understanding. Rather than multiple disconnected sūtra-cults, early Mahāyāna was a single, cohesive, primarily textual movement.

## 1. The "Many Mahāyānas" Hypothesis

* **Origin of the hypothesis**: In 1975, Gregory Schopen observed that early Mahāyāna sūtras often praise themselves in extravagant terms — claiming superiority over all other teachings or practices — and proposed that each text originated in its own separate "cult of the book" centred on a single manuscript.
* **Later elaboration**: Scholars such as Donald Lopez (2001), Jonathan Silk (2002, 2020), and Jan Nattier (2003) built on this observation, arguing that early Mahāyāna consisted of distinct, largely uncoordinated "Mahāyānas" (sometimes with mutually incompatible doctrines or practices) that only coalesced centuries later when the label "Mahāyāna" became widespread.

## 2. Endorsement of Mahāyāna Sūtras in the Plural

Drewes surveys a broad range of the earliest Mahāyāna sūtras — including the *Aṣṭasāhasrikā Prajñāpāramitā*, *Kāśyapaparivarta*, *Pratyutpanna*, larger *Sukhāvatīvyūha*, *Bhadrakalpika*, *Samādhirāja*, *Vimalakīrtinirdeśa*, *Pūrṇaparipṛcchā*, *Sāgaramatinirdeśa*, *Gaganagañja*, *Saddharmapuṇḍarīka* (*Lotus Sūtra*), *Suvikrāntavikrāmiparipṛcchā*, and Schøyen *Vajracchedikā* (*Diamond Sūtra*) — and shows that they consistently recognise and promote *other* Mahāyāna sūtras *in the plural*.

* **Pre-4th-century corpus terminology**: Although Seishi Karashima (2015) showed that the compound *Mahāyāna sūtra* itself only emerged around the fourth century CE, earlier texts already possessed a shared vocabulary for the genre as a whole:
  * **`evaṃrūpa`** ("sūtras of this kind" / "teachings such as these"), e.g. in the *Aṣṭasāhasrikā*, *Pratyutpanna*, and Schøyen *Vajracchedikā*;
  * **`vaipulya` / `vaitulya` / `*vevulla`** ("extensive" sūtras);
  * **`gambhīra`** ("profound" sūtras);
  * **`ṣaṭpāramitāpratisaṃyukta`** ("sūtras associated with the six perfections");
  * **`Bodhisattvapiṭaka`** ("the basket/collection of bodhisattva teachings").
* **Mutual reinforcement, not sectarian isolation**: Far from restricting devotion to a single text, these sūtras explicitly urge followers to seek out, hear, memorise, copy, and preach *all* such profound sūtras, and warn against rejecting any of them.

## 3. Explaining Self-Praise: Indian "Henotheism"

Why, then, do Mahāyāna sūtras simultaneously declare themselves supreme above all other teachings while endorsing other Mahāyāna sūtras as a class?

* **Parallel with Vedic and Purāṇic literature**: Drewes compares this rhetoric to what Max Müller termed **henotheism** in Vedic hymns and what David Shulman (1980) documented in Tamil *Sthalapurāṇas* (temple myths): each hymn or local shrine praises its own deity or sacred site as uniquely supreme without denying the legitimacy or supremacy of others in the wider tradition.
* **Promotional rhetoric, not sectarian exclusivity**: Extravagant self-praise in a Mahāyāna sūtra functioned as conventional Indian eulogy and advertising for that particular composition, not as evidence that its authors belonged to an exclusive single-sūtra cult.

## 4. A Shared Millenarian Identity

* **Return of the Bodhisattvas**: Across early Mahāyāna texts (*Pratyutpanna*, *Aṣṭasāhasrikā*, *Bhadrakalpika*, *Vimalakīrtinirdeśa*, *Suvikrāntavikramin*, larger *Sukhāvatīvyūha*), authors share a common millenarian scenario in which the Buddha predicts the future revelation of *multiple* Mahāyāna sūtras 500 years after his *parinirvāṇa* by an elect group of returning ancient bodhisattvas.
* **No evidence of competing sects**: These passages reflect awareness of a single textual corpus and a shared cosmic identity, with no awareness of schismatic institutions, lay revolts, or separate forest-ascetic subcultures.

## 5. Conclusion: One Cohesive Textual Movement

* **No single-sūtra exclusivists**: Drewes notes that no historical source — Indian, Chinese, or Tibetan — attests to any individual or group accepting only one Mahāyāna sūtra while rejecting others.
* **Yijing's definition**: Citing the seventh-century Chinese pilgrim Yijing's simple, empirical definition of Mahāyānists as "those who worship the Bodhisattvas and read the Mahāyāna Sūtras" while living within the same *nikāya* monastic lineages as non-Mahāyānists, Drewes concludes that early Mahāyāna is best understood as a single, cohesive, primarily textual movement driven by itinerant oral preachers (`dharmabhāṇakas`) operating across existing monastic communities.
