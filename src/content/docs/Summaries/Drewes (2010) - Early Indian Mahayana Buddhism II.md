## Abstract

Building on Part 1's critique of existing origin theories, David Drewes proposes a new framework for understanding early Indian Mahāyāna Buddhism. Rather than a splinter sect or ascetic subculture, early Mahāyāna is best understood as a textual movement centred on the composition, revelation, preaching, and dissemination of Mahāyāna sūtras within existing Buddhist institutional structures, driven primarily by specialist preachers called `dharmabhāṇakas`. An appendix evaluates taxonomic terms such as Mahāyāna, Theravāda, Hīnayāna, and "Mainstream Buddhism".

## 1. Reframing the Question: A Textual Movement

* **Sūtras, not the bodhisattva ideal, are the primary datum**: Conflating the origins of Mahāyāna with the origin of the bodhisattva ideal is misleading. Early Mahāyāna sūtras often present themselves as helpful for attaining `arhatship` and `pratyekabuddhahood` as well as Buddhahood, and introduce expanded cosmologies, pure lands, celestial Buddhas, new practices, and new philosophical perspectives.
* **Expanding the early Buddhist universe**: Drawing on the early canonical motif that the Buddha knew far more than he taught (such as the handful of leaves in the Siṃsapā grove), Mahāyāna authors used the sparsely defined bodhisattva figure as a framework to introduce new "special" sūtras (*vaipulya* or *gambhīra* sūtras) supposedly entrusted by the Buddha for revelation 500 years after his death.
* **No separate school or sect**: From Eugène Burnouf (1844) onwards, scholars repeatedly tried to assign Mahāyāna sūtras to a distinct school or ascetic group. Drewes argues that the simplest explanation for having hundreds of sūtras without a distinct sect is that early Indian Mahāyāna was primarily a textual movement operating within traditional *nikāya* structures, never acquiring a separate institutional identity in India.

## 2. Practices Advocated by Early Mahāyāna Sūtras

* **Text-centred practices over meditation or social service**: Early Mahāyāna sūtras rarely advocate social service, celestial bodhisattva worship, book shrines, or rigorous ascetic meditation. Instead, they overwhelmingly promote accessible methods for rapid progress towards Buddhahood:
  * Easy practices for rebirth in pure lands such as Abhirati or Sukhāvatī (hearing names of Buddhas/bodhisattvas, keeping precepts, listening to or memorising sūtras).
  * `Anumodanā` (rejoicing in the accumulated merit of all Buddhas and beings).
  * Above all, engaging directly with Mahāyāna sūtras — listening to, memorising, reciting, preaching, copying, and venerating them — promised to generate far greater merit than vast material offerings.
* **Neglect of textual practice in Western scholarship**: Western observers have historically equated "true Buddhism" with meditation and philosophy, overlooking that in South and Southeast Asian Buddhism, memorising, reciting, preaching, and listening to texts have consistently been central religious practices.

## 3. The `dharmabhāṇakas` as Primary Agents

* **A distinctively Mahāyāna preacher class**: Whereas non-Mahāyāna texts refer to reciters and preachers as `bhāṇakas` or `dharmakathikas`, Mahāyāna sūtras frequently name `dharmabhāṇakas` ("preachers of dharma") — e.g., 37 times in the *Aṣṭasāhasrikā*, 61 in the *Lotus*, and 49 in the Gilgit *Samādhirāja* — a term unattested in non-Mahāyāna Indian texts.
* **Role and glorification**: `Dharmabhāṇakas` memorised, recited, taught, and preached sūtras in monasteries, towns, and private homes, often identified as the future revealers (and likely authors) of the texts. Sūtras elevate them as irreversible bodhisattvas (at the ninth *bhūmi* in several texts) and urge extreme devotion towards them, including treating them as Buddhas and offering all one's property.
* **Not a reaction, and distinct from later *śāstras***: Early Mahāyānists were not reacting against non-Mahāyāna institutions; rather, they extended Buddhist narrative until critics pushed back. Later Sanskrit *śāstra* authors (Madhyamaka, Yogācāra) never call themselves `dharmabhāṇakas` and inhabited a different intellectual world, though their apologetics still focused specifically on defending Mahāyāna sūtras as authentic `buddhavacana`.
* **Early manuscript dates**: Recently discovered first-century CE manuscripts of early *Aṣṭasāhasrikā* chapters, an *Akṣobhyavyūha*-like sūtra, and the *Sucitti Sūtra* suggest Mahāyāna textual production may have begun as early as the first century BCE.

## 4. Appendix: Taxonomic Terms for Indian Buddhism

* **Mahāyāna vs. Theravāda**: Valid only after Buddhism disappeared from India. Theravāda emerged as a self-conscious lineage in Sri Lanka around the second–third century CE (after Mahāyāna had already begun), and early Theravādins often accepted Mahāyāna teachings until a tenth-century reform.
* **Hīnayāna / Nikāya / Sectarian Buddhism**: Inaccurate as labels for non-Mahāyāna because Mahāyānists themselves belonged to the *nikāyas*.
* **"Mainstream Buddhism"**: Coined by Eric Cheetham and popularised by Paul Harrison (1995), but now used inconsistently to mean non-Mahāyāna, the *nikāyas*, or all Buddhist communities; Drewes recommends dropping it.
* **Recommended usage**: Use **Mahāyāna** for people or groups who accept the authenticity of Mahāyāna sūtras (rather than everyone who identifies as a bodhisattva), **non-Mahāyāna** (or **traditional**) for those in ancient South Asia who did not, and **pre-Mahāyāna** or **early Buddhism** for Buddhism prior to Mahāyāna's emergence. Geographical labels ("Northern" vs. "Southern") fail historically because Mahāyāna was active as far south as Sri Lanka.
