## Abstract

Building on his earlier argument that there is no solid basis for treating the Buddha as a historical figure, David Drewes examines what happens to standard narratives of "early Buddhism" once that presupposition is dropped. He argues that the idea of the historical Buddha functioned as an unfalsifiable paradigm allowing scholars first to construct a nineteenth-century rationalist moral philosopher and then, under D. T. Suzuki's influence, a twentieth-century mysticism of meditation and ineffable religious experience — a "Buddhism that never was" contradicted by both early texts and pre-modern history.

## 1. The Historical Buddha as an Interpretive Gap

* **Supernatural earliest sources**: Even the earliest Buddhist texts depict the Buddha as a glorious, omniscient, supernatural being who flies and teleports.
* **The "original vs. accretion" hermeneutic**: Presuming a historical human behind these texts created an unfalsifiable "magical gap" beneath the earliest evidence. Scholars could cherry-pick congenial material as "original" and dismiss cosmology, gods, miracles, and ritual as later corruptions, popular religion, or lay accretions.

## 2. Phase 1: The Nineteenth-Century Rational Philosopher (Without Meditation)

* **Moral philosophy, not mystical experience**: Following Eugène Burnouf, nineteenth-century scholars (Max Müller, T. W. Rhys Davids, Hermann Oldenberg, J. Barthélemy Saint-Hilaire, and later Louis de La Vallée Poussin) portrayed the Buddha as a rational moral philosopher akin to an ancient Greek thinker.
* **Meditation dismissed**: Early scholars did *not* see meditation or ineffable religious experience as central. Müller called meditative states "hallucinations" "made for a mad house"; Rhys Davids judged `jhāna` to be of "small practical importance"; Oldenberg attributed meditative visions to nervous exhaustion; and La Vallée Poussin wrote that meditation, like asceticism, "is not an essential part of the Path".
* **Awakening as intellectual resolve**: Saint-Hilaire, Rhys Davids, and Oldenberg depicted the Buddha's awakening not as a mystical transformation, but as a philosopher's struggle with doubt culminating in intellectual certainty about his system.

## 3. Phase 2: D. T. Suzuki and the Ineffable Experience Paradigm

* **The Suzukian revolution**: D. T. Suzuki fused Rinzai Zen *satori* with William James's concept of ineffable, noetic religious experience and projected it back onto Indian Buddhism, presenting awakening as a replicable, transformative state of consciousness.
* **Adoption in Buddhist studies**: Figures such as Christmas Humphreys, Edward Conze, and Walpola Rahula embraced Suzuki's framework, reading Zen back into the Pāḷi Canon (with Rahula equating *satori* with `arhatship`). By the 1980s–1990s, the "ineffable experience" paradigm became taken for granted — even underlying newer theories like the "forest hypothesis" of early Mahāyāna.
* **Overwriting the texts**: Because early texts do not describe an ineffable experience, Suzuki dismissed early disciples as "jackals" unable to comprehend a lion, urging readers to "look underneath" the texts. Drewes notes that later scholars (Tilmann Vetter, K. R. Norman, Richard Gombrich, Johannes Bronkhorst, Anālayo) similarly had to treat canonical descriptions of awakening (such as the Four Realisations) as later conceptualisations of an allegedly ineffable or non-conceptual event.
* **What the texts actually say**: Following Lambert Schmithausen, Drewes observes that early texts show no difficulty verbalising awakening: the standard account describes three watches of the night — recalling past lives, seeing beings' karma and rebirth, and realising the Four Realisations and the destruction of defilements — a revelation of metaphysical truths about death and the afterlife that served as boilerplate justification for a *śramaṇa* tradition's truth claims.

## 4. Meditation and `Arahantship` in Historical Practice

* **Little canonical or historical evidence for widespread meditation**: In early texts, most disciples attain liberation simply by listening to a discourse; meditative states are correlated with supernatural powers and divine realms, without practical how-to instructions.
* **Exegetical treatises, not practice manuals**: Following Robert Sharf, Drewes notes that later treatises such as Buddhaghosa's *Visuddhimagga* are scriptural exegesis rather than records of lived practice — and Buddhaghosa himself states that only one in a million to a billion people can attain even the first *jhāna*.
* **Absence in pre-modern reports**: Neither fifth–seventh-century Chinese pilgrims to India nor seventeenth–nineteenth-century Western visitors to Theravāda lands reported observing meditation. A survey commissioned by King Rama V in Thailand found no living monk had attained any *jhāna*, and modern *vipassanā* movements originated in the twentieth century.
* **`Arahantship` always in the past**: Pre-modern Buddhists widely held that the Dharma had deteriorated and that `arhatship` had become unattainable until the next Buddha, Maitreya (e.g., I. B. Horner 1936; Sri Lankan tradition placing the last `arahant`, Maliyadeva, in the second century BCE; George Bond 1984; *Dazhidu lun*; Faxian's fifth-century report of a rare presumed `arahant` in Sri Lanka).

## 5. Conclusion

Without the presupposition of a historical Buddha to anchor Suzukian awakening and meditation at Buddhism's origin, the modern vision of a primordial "golden age" of universal psychological transformation collapses. Drewes urges scholars to approach early Buddhism realistically as an ancient religion — comparable to ancient Egyptian, Greek, Jain, or Ājīvika traditions — centred on a supernatural figure, metaphysical truths, ritual practices, and afterlife concerns, rather than a timeless repository of modern insights.
