## Abstract

Because Mahāyāna sūtras claim to be the word of the Buddha (`buddhavacana`) yet appeared centuries after earlier sūtras, their authors needed to explain their sudden emergence. David Drewes surveys a widespread motif he calls the **"Return of the Bodhisattvas"** — in which the Buddha entrusts Mahāyāna sūtras to great bodhisattvas who vow to return to this world at the time of the Dharma's disappearance (*paścima kāla*) to reveal and spread them — and argues this millenarian vision was a central belief of early Mahāyāna. He also evaluates two other scholarly explanations: `śrāvaka` "inspired speech" (`pratibhāna`) and meditative/dream visions of other-world Buddhas.

## 1. The "Return of the Bodhisattvas" Scenario

* **Locus classicus (*Pratyutpanna Sūtra*)**: Studied by Paul Harrison, the *Pratyutpanna* depicts the Buddha predicting that the text will circulate for 40 years after his *parinirvāṇa*, be hidden in caves, stūpas, earth, and with gods and *nāgas*, and then be reacquired and spread during the "last time" (*paścima kāla*) — the ruin of the True Dharma (*saddharmasya antardhāna*) — by Bhadrapāla and 500 returning bodhisattvas who heard it from the Buddha himself. Its authors thus authenticated both the sūtra and themselves as reincarnations of those bodhisattvas.
* **Widespread across early Mahāyāna**: Drewes demonstrates that this scenario is not unique to the *Pratyutpanna* but appears as a standard authenticating motif across early and middle Mahāyāna literature — including the *Aṣṭasāhasrikā Prajñāpāramitā* (chs. 3 and 10), *Bhadrakalpika*, *Vimalakīrtinirdeśa*, *Suvikrāntavikramiparipṛcchā*, larger *Sukhāvatīvyūha*, Schøyen *Vajracchedikā* (*Diamond Sūtra*), *Lokānuvartana*, *Samādhirāja*, *Drumakinnararājaparipṛcchā*, *Ajātaśatrukaukṛtyavinodana*, *Śūraṃgamasamādhi*, *Saddharmapuṇḍarīka* (*Lotus Sūtra*), *Akṣayamatinirdeśa*, and *Sarvadharmaguṇavyūharāja*. Several assign Maitreya (or Śakra) a sustaining role (*adhiṣṭhāna*) in delivering the texts to returning bodhisattvas.
* **Two chronological layers**: Earlier passages use *paścima kāla* simply to mean the final days while the Buddha's Dharma is disappearing; later passages define *paścima kāla* as a distinct second 500-year period — likely after Mahāyānists realised more than 500 years had passed since the Buddha's death.

## 2. Historical Implications for Early Mahāyāna

* **A single textual corpus**: Many passages predict the revelation of Mahāyāna sūtras *in the plural* ("sūtras of this kind", *vaipulya* sūtras, "sūtras associated with the six perfections"), refuting the idea that early Mahāyāna consisted of disconnected single-sūtra cults.
* **A millenarian, textual movement**: Return of the Bodhisattvas passages are the only passages reflecting awareness of a broader Mahāyāna movement — and they show no awareness of schismatic sects, lay revolts, forest-ascetic subcultures, or people choosing to become bodhisattvas in this life. Instead, early Mahāyānists saw themselves as a cosmic elect of ancient bodhisattvas returning in a millenarian age to reveal a textual corpus.

## 3. Alternative Explanations Evaluated

* **`Pratibhāna` ("inspired speech") of `śrāvakas` (Graeme MacQueen)**: At the opening of the *Aṣṭasāhasrikā*, Subhūti teaches *Prajñāpāramitā* through inspired speech (`pratibhāna`), echoing the Pāḷi *Uttaravipattisutta*'s principle that "whatever is well spoken is the word of the Buddha" (*extended buddhavacana*). Drewes notes that while MacQueen overstates his case in claiming Mahāyāna sūtras did not claim to be spoken by the Buddha (since Return of the Bodhisattvas passages explicitly do), the unusual role of a `śrāvaka` (`dharmakathika`, never called a `dharmabhāṇaka`) instructing bodhisattvas may preserve a trace of an early pre-sūtra phase in which *Prajñāpāramitā* ideas circulated in `śrāvaka` circles (cf. Tilmann Vetter 2001).
* **Meditation and dream revelations (Paul Harrison & Jan Nattier)**: Based on a single passage in the *Pratyutpanna* describing a seven-day visualisation of Amitāyus yielding a sūtra in a vision or dream, Harrison and Nattier suggested Mahāyāna sūtras originated as residues of visionary *samādhi* experiences. Drewes rejects this:
  * The *Pratyutpanna* itself explains its own origin via the Return of the Bodhisattvas, not meditation; no known Mahāyāna sūtra claims to have been received from another-world Buddha in meditation or dreams (all present themselves as spoken by Śākyamuni).
  * The *Pratyutpanna*'s method is a one-off suggestion that never caught on, and the *Sukhāvatīvyūha* does not advocate meditation at all.
  * Composing sūtras in an ordinary state of mind using stock phrasing and conventions is far simpler and more plausible than attributing them to meditative hallucinations; generally, Mahāyāna sūtras show little concern for actual meditation practice.
