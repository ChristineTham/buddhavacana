## Abstract

Challenging the long-standing scholarly assumption that early Buddhists could simply choose to become bodhisattvas out of altruism or as a "vocational alternative", David Drewes argues that across all known *nikāya* traditions — and in early Mahāyāna itself — entering the bodhisattva path required the presence of a living Buddha, and certainty required a Buddha's prediction (*vyākaraṇa*). Because no Buddha was alive in the world, becoming a bodhisattva in one's present life was considered impossible. Early Mahāyāna sūtra authors overcame this obstacle not by urging listeners to start the path from scratch, but by declaring that anyone who encountered and believed in their sūtras had *already* become an advanced or irreversible bodhisattva in past lives — thereby bringing a coherent bodhisattva tradition into existence for the first time.

## 1. *Nikāya* Models: The Requirement of a Living Buddha

* **The Dīpaṃkara prototype**: All known models of the bodhisattva path derive from the story of the future Buddha (Sumedha/Megha/Sumati) making a resolution (*abhinīhāra* / *praṇidhāna*) before the living Buddha Dīpaṃkara and receiving his prediction (*vyākaraṇa*).
* **Theravāda**: The *Buddhavaṃsa* lists eight conditions for a valid resolution, including the presence of a living Buddha (*satthāradassana*); Dhammapāla and later Theravāda commentators maintain that one becomes a `bodhisatta` (and irreversible) only upon receiving a Buddha's prediction. Later models (*Jinakālamālī*) extend the path back through mental and verbal resolutions, but still exclude any initial aspiration made outside a Buddha's presence (*buddhadassanavirahita*).
* **Sarvāstivāda, Mūlasarvāstivāda, and Mahāsāṃghika-Lokottaravāda**: The *Mahāvibhāṣā*, *Abhidharmakośabhāṣya*, Mūlasarvāstivāda *Vinayavastu*, and *Mahāvastu* all require entering the path in a Buddha's presence and treat eventual Buddhahood as uncertain until a Buddha's prediction is received.
* **Explicit prohibitions**: Commentaries such as the *Nidānakathā*, *Buddhavaṃsa*, and *Cariyāpiṭaka* commentaries reject substitute resolutions before *stūpas*, bodhi trees, images, or disciples, while the *Mahāvibhāṣā* warns against false self-conceit. New resolutions made in this life were regarded as almost certain to fail (illustrated by Ledi Sayadaw, the *Dazhidu lun*'s simile of fish eggs, and Ajahn Mun abandoning a past bodhisattva vow).

## 2. Workarounds in Theravāda and Non-Mahāyāna Traditions

* **Aiming for the next Buddha (Metteyya/Maitreya)**: From the *Jātaka* commentary and twelfth-century Pagan inscriptions (King Alaungsithu) to modern figures such as Anagarika Dharmapala and Burmese Prime Minister U Nu, aspirants accumulated vast merit hoping to meet Maitreya in a future life and receive a prediction from him.
* **Claiming past-life bodhisattva status**: Drawing on *jātaka* precedents where a bodhisattva is unaware of his own status, some Theravāda kings, monks (such as Nārada Thera and fourteenth-century Thai monk Srīsradharājacūlāmūnī), and twentieth-century Burmese esoteric groups (Ariyā-weizzā) claimed or sought to verify past-life bodhisattva status through kingship or miraculous "acts of truth".

## 3. Early Mahāyāna's Text-Based Solution

* **Sharing the same presupposition**: Early Mahāyāna sūtras never encourage listeners to become bodhisattvas from scratch or provide an initiation ritual. Like *nikāya* texts, they depict initial *bodhicitta* as arising in a Buddha's presence and scorn "beginner" (*ādikarmika*) bodhisattvas as weak, unintelligent, and likely to fall back to the `śrāvaka` or `pratyekabuddha` level.
* **Divinatory and dream tests**: The *Aṣṭasāhasrikā Prajñāpāramitā* mentions early methods for testing whether one is already an irreversible (*avinivartanīya*) bodhisattva — doctrinal tests, dreams of teaching the Dharma or seeing all phenomena as dreamlike, and acts of truth extinguishing fires or exorcising spirits — though it warns that Māra can interfere with such tests.
* **Reaction to the sūtra as proof of status**: Far more pervasively, the *Aṣṭasāhasrikā* (including the oldest datable Split Collection fragment) and numerous other early sūtras (*Bajaur Mahāyāna Sūtra*, *Akṣobhyavyūha*, *Pratyutpanna*, *Drumakinnararājaparipṛcchā*, *Śūraṃgamasamādhi*, larger and smaller *Sukhāvatīvyūha*, *Vimalakīrtinirdeśa*, *Akṣayamatinirdeśa*) declare that anyone who hears, believes in, memorises, recites, copies, or does not shrink back from the sūtra is *already* an advanced or irreversible bodhisattva who has worshipped past Buddhas — or becomes irreversible immediately upon hearing it.

## 4. Historical Implications for the Emergence of Mahāyāna

* **Not later "democratisation" or flattery**: Against Paul Harrison's reading of these passages as rhetorical flattery (*captatio benevolentiae*) and Jan Nattier's reading of them as a later "democratisation" of an originally gruelling ascetic path, Drewes notes that irreversibility claims appear in the very oldest datable Mahāyāna texts and were the primary device allowing early followers to identify as bodhisattvas at all.
* **Later developments**: Over time, Mahāyāna authors broadened bodhisattva attribution through the *ekayāna* ("one vehicle") doctrine and *gotra* (lineage) theory, eventually producing formal rituals for undertaking the bodhisattva path in this life (first attested in the late third-century *Bodhisattvabhūmi*).
* **Conclusion**: Rather than emerging from a pre-existing bodhisattva movement or an ethical "vocational choice", Mahāyāna sūtras themselves brought a coherent bodhisattva tradition into existence by using the infallible authority of sūtra revelation to certify their audiences as long-established bodhisattvas.

*(Note: Drewes appends a brief correction on p. 29 of the preprint noting that his statement in the body text attributing to Yaśomitra the view that one becomes a bodhisattva only in one's final life was mistaken, though the Abhidharmadīpa commentary indicates some Abhidharmikas did hold that view.)*
